The question

Over the past few years, one failure after another — a data-center fire that took a nation's chat app offline, a telecom breach exposing 23 million people, a faulty update that grounded flights worldwide — cut off services millions rely on. The obvious question is whether attacks are rising. The harder one, and the one this study asks: has our ability to defend kept pace? Answering it means measuring both sides — the pressure, and the capacity built to absorb it.

The through-line

The two widest cascades in the study — MOVEit and CrowdStrike, hitting eight and seven of eight sectors — produced no new binding rule. The narrowest did. Regulation doesn't track how far a failure spreads. It tracks whether personal data leaked inside a sector a regulator already polices.

The framework

The whole study reads through one idea, the efficiency–fragility tradeoff: the same design choices that make digital systems cheaper and faster also make them more fragile. Moving everyone's services onto a few cloud providers cuts costs — but it means a single break can take down far more than before. Fragility isn't the price you pay to get efficiency. It's part of what efficiency is.

That idea predicts exactly what the capacity analysis finds. Concentration is not a flaw any single company can be cited for, so a regulatory system built to police data breaches simply can't see it. The pressure grows along one axis — structure. The defense is lined up along another — confidentiality. And the two never meet.
