Measure 04 · Capacity

What institutions actually respond to

The first three measures show the pressure rising. This one asks the other half of the question: when a major failure hits, how fast and how far does the response reach — and does it touch the thing that made the failure systemic?

The pattern across six cases

Every response comes after the failure, never before it. Firms rebuild their own defenses — but only their own. And the size of the regulatory response has no relationship to how far the failure spread. What predicts a new rule isn't damage. It's whether personal data leaked inside a sector a regulator already polices.

Six of the twelve cascade incidents are examined as matched pairs — each linking one serious failure to the regulatory or enforcement action that followed. For each: did the firm respond, did a binding rule follow, and how long did it take? Read them in order and the pattern surfaces on its own.

Why the two extremes are the whole argument

Line up the widest cascades against the narrowest. MOVEit and CrowdStrike caused the broadest damage in the entire study — and neither produced a single new binding rule. Kakao, the narrowest in cross-sector terms, produced the only new law. If regulation tracked systemic disruption, this would be upside down.

It isn't tracking disruption. It's tracking data exposure inside a sector a regulator already oversees. SK Telecom exposed subscriber IDs, and a data-protection law was there to use. Change Healthcare exposed health records, and HIPAA was there. CrowdStrike exposed no data and named no attacker — so no tool was reached for at all, even for the largest outage on record.

This is the efficiency–fragility tradeoff showing up in the institutional record instead of in theory. Firms can fix their own flaws, but a firm can't fix its own centrality. Concentration isn't a defect any single company can be cited for — so a regulatory system built to police data breaches simply can't see it. The pressure grows along one axis. The defense is arrayed along another. And that, not slowness, is the gap.
